I think the teapot hold is the most dangerous move in the set of fundamental contra dancing figures, so I've been avoiding it in my dancing. While it might seem innocuous at first, the teapot hold requires one of the dancers to connect with their hand in the small of their back. This rotates the shoulder to a less stable position, where it's less supported by muscles and more likely to be injured if tugged. When I realized that I could avoid putting my shoulder into this vulnerable position, my contra dancing immediately felt safer.
---
## What is the Teapot Hold
In a teapot hold, sometimes called a "courtesy turn hold", two dancers connect right-hand in right-hand and left-hand in left-hand and stand side-by-side. One dancer places a hand behind themselves in the small of their back, and one in front, and the other dancer connects with them as shown below [^1]. In New England, where I dance most often, the teapot hold is used both for the courtesy turn, where the couple rotates to face the opposite direction, and also in promenade, where the couple walks forward. Elsewhere, promenade might default to skater's or butterfly.

## Why I don't like the Teapot Hold
There's a number of reasons I don't like the teapot hold.

### It puts your shoulder in an unsupported position, vulnerable to injury
There's no way to guarantee you won't get injured while social dancing, but there's a lot you can do as a dancer to minimize risk. One big one is to dance in such a way that you keep your joints aligned and supported by the musculature surrounding them. Part of this is keeping shoulders stably seated in the socket by avoiding over-extending arms or twisting into positions where the arm is supported mostly by connective tissue rather than muscle. This helps keep the shoulder supported and dynamic, with muscles able to absorb an unexpected tug or push. 

In every basic contra figure except for the teapot hold, it's possible for all of the dancers to keep their arms in front of them. Unfortunately, the teapot hold requires twisting the arm around and losing a lot of that support. If someone tugs your arm while it's in this position, there's not much you can do to protect yourself from injury.

### It requires significant shoulder mobility
Rotating your shoulder around so that you can reach the small of your back is something many of us take for granted, but it's actually a relatively difficult motion. Especially for many older adults with less range of motion or people with shoulder injuries in their past, this might not be possible for them to do [^2]. 

Once I realized this, I kept noticing it: more often than not, there will be someone in the beginner lesson who cannot put their hand far enough behind their back during the courtesy turn or promenade. Contra as an intergenerational dance is very important to me, and I don't like that one of the basic figures requires a range of motion that older adults relatively commonly have lost.

### It invites connection near the small of the back
The small of the back is a relatively intimate location. There are many people who I am totally comfortable having their hand on my shoulderblade, who I would not want feeling my lower back. In theory, the teapot hold is hand-to-hand and no small of back touching should happen. But, the number of times I've had someone reach for my hand and miss is not small, even though I currently have full range of motion and can put my hand there just fine. It probably happens even more for folks who struggle to put their hand there. Ideally, upon not catching the hand would float above the back, disconnected, since the main connection point of a courtesy turn or promenade is the hands in front anyways. But in reality, unwanted lower back touches do happen. If we didn't have a move with a connection point in the small of the back in our basic figures, we'd significantly reduce this problem.
