I think the teapot hold is the most dangerous basic contra dancing figure, so I've been avoiding it. While it might seem innocuous, the teapot hold requires one dancer to connect with their hand in the small of their back. This rotates the shoulder to a less stable position where it's less supported by muscles and more likely to be injured if tugged. When I realized I could avoid putting my shoulder into this vulnerable position, my contra dancing immediately felt safer.
---

## What is the Teapot Hold
In a teapot hold, sometimes called a "courtesy turn hold", two dancers connect right-hand in right-hand and left-hand in left-hand and stand side-by-side. One dancer places a hand behind themselves in the small of their back, and one in front, and the other dancer connects with them as shown below [^1]. In New England, where I dance most often, the teapot hold is used both for the courtesy turn, where the couple rotates to face the opposite direction, and also in promenade, where the couple walks forward. Elsewhere, promenade might default to skater's or butterfly.

## Why I don't like the Teapot Hold

### It puts your shoulder in an unsupported position
There's no way to guarantee you won't get injured while social dancing, but there's a lot you can do to minimize risk. One big one is keeping your joints aligned and supported by surrounding musculature. An example of this is keeping shoulders stably seated in the socket by avoiding over-extending arms or twisting into positions where the arm is supported mostly by connective tissue rather than muscle. This helps keep the shoulder supported and dynamic, with muscles able to absorb an unexpected tug or push. 

In every basic contra figure except the teapot hold, all dancers can keep their arms in front of them. Unfortunately, the teapot hold requires twisting the arm around and losing a lot of that support. If someone tugs your arm while it's in this position, there's not much you can do to protect yourself from injury.

### It requires significant shoulder mobility
Rotating your shoulder around to reach the small of your back is something many of us take for granted, but it's actually a relatively difficult motion. Especially for older adults with less range of motion or people with past shoulder injuries, this might not be possible [^2]. 

Once I realized this, I kept noticing it: more often than not, there will be someone in the beginner lesson who cannot put their hand far enough behind their back during the courtesy turn or promenade. Contra as an intergenerational dance is very important to me, and I don't like that one of the basic figures requires a range of motion that older adults commonly lose.

### It invites connection near the small of the back
The small of the back is a relatively intimate location. There are many people I'm totally comfortable having their hand on my shoulderblade, who I would not want feeling my lower back. In theory, the teapot hold is hand-to-hand and no small of back touching should happen. But the number of times I've had someone reach for my hand and miss is not small, even though I currently have full range of motion. It probably happens even more for folks who struggle to put their hand there. Ideally, upon not catching the hand would float above the back, disconnected, since the main connection point is the hands in front anyways. But in reality, unwanted lower back touches do happen. If we didn't have a move with a connection point in the small of the back in our basic figures, we'd significantly reduce this problem.

## Replacing the Teapot Hold
I have spent the last few months avoiding the teapot hold, offering skater's everywhere, and it has gone really well.

The key to why it works so well is that in teapot hold, the most important connection point is the hands in front. This connection is unchanged when switching to skater's - the hands in front are in the same place as before. You can think about an open chain, where the hands in front are the only connection point during the courtesy turn - mechanically it all works with only one hand. So the location of the secondary hands is less impactful on the success of the overall figure. If it connects, great! If it doesn't, no biggie, you are still dancing. And in my experience, offering skater's clearly in front usually does connect just fine.

Occasionally, it doesn't go perfectly. In one case, a person was confused thinking I was offering a twirl they didn't recognize. Sometimes people don't notice, and so only the single hand always in front connects as in an open chain. But most of the time, it just works. Both experienced and new dancers seem to roll with the difference just fine. New dancers are a bit overwhelmed with so many new-to-them things on the floor anyways, that one more thing isn't a big deal - they are already primarily reacting in the moment. An offer of holding hands is clear and they connect in the obvious way without issue. More experienced dancers are used to following the unexpected, and have often seen skater's hold before, so adjust easily.

I was a bit worried at first that changing to always offering skater's would mean more small-of-back touches, as people put their hand there out of habit and then my hand wouldn't be able to catch theirs. In practice, this hasn't been the case; if anything it has slightly decreased because I'm so clearly inviting connection in front instead.

Overall, the small handful of briefly confused folks feels very worth it to avoid dancing with my shoulder in an unstable position. I hope to continue dancing for many years to come, and making small changes to reduce chance of injury on the floor helps make the odds of that higher. I plan to continue with this change and recommend anyone interested to try it out.
